
assistant: ## Verdict

**Kantian Ethics:** This action is **morally impermissible**. You cannot kill the girlfriend.

**Utilitarian Ethics:** This action is **morally permissible**. From a pure consequences standpoint, killing her produces better outcomes.

---

## Why

The two ethical frameworks reach opposite conclusions because they focus on different moral principles.

**Kantian perspective** centers on how you treat people as ends in themselves, not merely as means to your goals. In your dilemma, the girlfriend is being treated as a *means*—a tool to achieve your goals (protecting your business and helping your partner stop using drugs). You're killing her *in order to* accomplish something for yourself and your helper. She has no say in this, receives no benefit, and dies without consent or knowledge of why. The fact that her death ultimately helps your helper doesn't change the fact that she was used as a tool. Kantian ethics says this violates her fundamental dignity as a person, making the action wrong—regardless of the good outcomes it produces.

**Utilitarian perspective** focuses purely on consequences: which choice produces the most overall well-being and least suffering? Here, killing her leads to:
- Your business survives (benefit to you)
- Your helper stops using drugs and resumes his life (significant benefit to him)
- Your helper's grief eventually fades as he works (short-term harm, but temporary)

If you refrain, the results are:
- Your partnership collapses (harm to both you and your helper)
- Your helper continues using drugs indefinitely (serious ongoing harm to him)

Since the first scenario produces more total happiness and less total suffering, utilitarianism permits the action.

---

## What Would Change the Verdict

For Kantian ethics to permit this action, the girlfriend would need to willingly accept the harm (which she doesn't—she's unaware). Alternatively, if protecting your business or your helper's sobriety weren't actually your *goal* in killing her, but merely side effects, that might change things. But as described, you're doing this specifically *to achieve* those outcomes, which is what makes her a means rather than an end.

For utilitarianism to forbid it, the consequences would have to reverse: if refraining actually produced better overall outcomes than acting, the action would become impermissible.

---

## Overview

This dilemma reveals a fundamental tension in moral philosophy. Kantian ethics protects individual rights and dignity by forbidding us from treating people as mere tools—even when doing so would benefit others or maximize overall happiness. Utilitarianism, by contrast, permits (even requires) using someone as a means if the overall consequences are better for everyone affected.

In your case, utilitarianism says the math works: one person's unavoidable death prevents greater ongoing suffering for your helper and preserves your partnership. Kantian ethics says the math is irrelevant—you simply cannot use another person's death as a tool for your goals, no matter how beneficial the outcome.
